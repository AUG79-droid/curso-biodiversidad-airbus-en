Module 1 · 20–30 minutes

Sustainability, nature and business

Sustainability is not a collection of environmental actions. It is the capacity to preserve value and fulfil a mission without degrading the social, economic and ecological conditions on which that activity depends.

Objectives

Key idea

Nature is not the backdrop

An aircraft may appear to be a wholly technological system. Yet its materials, logistics chain, operating infrastructure and the physical stability of the places involved all depend on natural systems. The useful question is not whether a company “loves nature”, but where it depends on nature, where it changes nature and which decisions reduce risk.

Module rule

Before promising improvement, locate the interface with nature and understand its mechanism.

Theory 1.1

Four concepts that should not be confused

Keeping them distinct improves analysis and prevents every “green” initiative from appearing equivalent.

Nature

Nature encompasses the planet's living and non-living components and the interactions among them. It includes organisms, soils, water, the atmosphere, biogeochemical cycles and physical processes. It is the wider system within which the economy operates.

At an industrial site, nature includes a community of birds or pollinators as well as soil dynamics, runoff and connectivity with other habitats.

Biodiversity

Biodiversity is the variability of life: genetic diversity within populations, species diversity and ecosystem diversity. Relationships also matter, including predation, pollination, seed dispersal, decomposition and competition.

Counting species provides information, but it is not enough. A site may retain the same number of species while losing specialists, connectivity or essential ecological functions.

Climate

Climate influences species distributions, water availability and the frequency of disturbance. In turn, healthy ecosystems store carbon, regulate temperature and buffer extreme events. The two issues are connected, but they are not interchangeable.

A climate solution may damage biodiversity if it occupies valuable habitat or increases pressure on minerals, water or soil. A multi-criteria assessment is therefore stronger than a decision based on a single indicator.

Sustainability

Sustainability is a framework for decisions that considers environmental, social, operational and economic effects over time. It does not mean eliminating every impact; it means recognising limits, avoiding unnecessary harm, managing trade-offs and justifying priorities with evidence.

In aerospace, sustainability must coexist with airworthiness, safety, availability, mission and compliance. Integration does not weaken those requirements; it improves the quality of the overall decision.

Theory 1.2

From ecological interaction to business risk

Dependencies

A dependency exists when an organisation needs a contribution from nature. It may be physical — water of a particular quality, stable ground or raw materials — or regulatory, such as flood control, temperature moderation or soil regeneration. Some dependencies are local; others are hidden several tiers upstream in the supply chain.

A dependency does not automatically indicate poor practice. It identifies a condition that must be understood because deterioration can affect cost, continuity, quality or availability.

Impacts

An impact is a change in nature associated with an activity. It may occur in direct operations or across the value chain through land transformation, fragmentation, extraction, discharges, noise, artificial light at night, species introduction or pressure on resources.

Robust analysis describes the mechanism, location, intensity, duration, reversibility and affected receptor. “It has an impact” is too imprecise to guide a decision.

Risks and opportunities

Impacts and dependencies may translate into physical, regulatory, transition, reputational or operational risks. A flood that interrupts access is a physical risk; an untraceable mineral that becomes unacceptable is a supply-chain risk; an unsupported environmental claim creates legal and reputational risk.

Opportunities also emerge: repairable design, contracts with stronger traceability, maintenance that extends service life, restoration that reduces a local pressure or data systems that anticipate conflict.

Double materiality

Impact materiality asks how the activity affects people and nature. Financial materiality asks how environmental issues may affect organisational performance and value. The same interaction may be material from both perspectives.

For example, degrading natural drainage may affect habitat while also increasing a facility's exposure to flooding. The first effect must not be reduced to the second: both matter and are documented separately.

Theory 1.3

The five direct drivers of biodiversity loss

IPBES groups the main global mechanisms. In a specific project, they must be translated into observable, location-specific pressures.

  • Land- and sea-use change: habitat conversion, sealing, fragmentation or modification. In an industrial setting it can result from extensions, access routes, logistics zones or supporting infrastructure.
  • Direct exploitation of organisms: timber extraction, fishing, use of wild species or biological raw materials. It may occur far from the site, within suppliers and sub-suppliers.
  • Climate change: shifts ranges, phenology, mortality and disturbance frequency. It is a cross-cutting driver, but does not replace analysis of the others.
  • Pollution: substances, nutrients, particles, noise or light that alter ecological condition. The exposure pathway and receptor determine significance.
  • Invasive alien species: introduced organisms that establish, spread and cause effects. Packaging, equipment movement, landscaping and transport can create introduction pathways.

Business analysis must go beyond naming these drivers. It must locate where pressures arise, which receptors they reach, which controls exist and what evidence shows whether the pressure is decreasing.

Theory 1.4 · Deep dive

Turning a broad topic into a manageable decision

Sustainability becomes embedded in a programme only when it is translated into questions that engineering, procurement, operations and management can answer.

1. Define the decision

The starting point is not “improve biodiversity”, but a specific decision: select a site, choose an architecture, award a supplier, schedule a test or approve a communication. The required function, available alternatives, non-negotiable requirements and the point at which genuine influence still exists must all be stated.

If analysis begins after design and contract freeze, most mitigation-hierarchy options have already been lost. Early integration does not guarantee an impact-free option, but it preserves avoidance choices and reduces the cost of correction.

2. Locate the interface with nature

Interactions are not uniform. One purchase may involve extraction in a water-stressed basin, refining near a sensitive area, manufacturing on industrial land and use at a base with very different receptors. Location changes the ecological, social and regulatory context.

An aggregated inventory of tonnes or spend is therefore insufficient for biodiversity. Activity, geography, pressure, receptor and period must be linked. When location is unknown, the gap must be recorded as a data limitation and a decision risk.

3. Assess significance

Priority combines magnitude, extent, duration, frequency, reversibility, receptor sensitivity, accumulation with other pressures and uncertainty. A small impact on a critical receptor may require more attention than a larger disturbance on severely degraded land.

Uncertainty does not mean low impact. If missing data could change the choice, investigate, condition approval or apply proportionate precaution. The risk matrix should explain the reasoning rather than conceal it behind a score.

4. Assign governance

Every material issue needs an owner, evidence, due date and escalation rule. Engineering controls requirements and architecture; procurement can flow down obligations and audit rights; operations implements controls and records deviations; environment provides methods and monitoring; communications verifies that claims do not exceed the evidence.

A mature decision retains its record: rejected alternatives, assumptions, sources, accountable owners and approval rationale. This traceability enables learning, supports audits and allows criteria to be revisited when evidence or conditions change.

Minimum management chain

Decision → location → dependency or impact → pressure and receptor → significance → alternative → control → indicator → threshold → owner. If one link is missing, the proposal may still be valid, but it has not yet been explained sufficiently to qualify as an evidence-based sustainability decision.

Module 2 · 25–35 minutes

How biodiversity works

Move beyond “counting animals and plants” to understand composition, structure, functions, connectivity and ecological change.

Key idea

A list does not describe a system

Two areas may each support twenty species and still have very different ecological value. It matters which species are present, how many individuals persist, whether they can reproduce, whether habitat is connected and whether the processes sustaining the community still function.

Module rule

Biodiversity must be interpreted in spatial and temporal context; a single snapshot does not demonstrate a trend.

Theory 2.1

Three levels, one network

Genetic diversity

This is heritable variation within a species. It enables populations to respond to disease, climate change and new conditions. A large population may still be vulnerable if it descends from few individuals or remains isolated for many generations.

Corporate management rarely measures it directly, but it must be understood: maintaining connectivity and viable populations helps preserve genetic exchange and adaptive capacity.

Species diversity

It includes richness — how many species — and evenness — how individuals are distributed. Identity also matters: native, threatened, sensitive, invasive or functionally important species.

Increasing richness is not always positive. Generalist or non-native species may enter degraded habitat while specialists disappear. Composition must therefore be interpreted alongside abundance and habitat condition.

Ecosystem diversity

This describes the variety of habitats, communities and processes in a landscape. A mosaic of grassland, scrub, temporary wetland and bare ground may support more functions than a uniform cover, even if the latter appears visually “greener”.

Administrative and ecological boundaries do not coincide. A site belongs to a catchment, corridor, urban or agricultural matrix and wildlife movement routes.

Interactions and functions

Pollination, dispersal, predation, decomposition, soil formation and infiltration emerge from relationships among organisms and the physical environment. If a key interaction disappears, the system may lose function before total species numbers change visibly.

A robust assessment therefore asks not only “what is here?” but also “what is happening, and can the system sustain itself?”

Theory 2.2

Composition, structure and function

These three lenses describe ecological condition without reducing it to one number.

Composition

Which elements form the system: species, functional groups, communities and habitat types? A community dominated by a few opportunistic species may indicate simplification even where richness remains relatively high.

Structure

How is the system organised physically and spatially: vegetation layers, age, patch size, edges, continuity, refuges and relationships among habitats? Structure determines which species can feed, reproduce or move.

Function

Which processes operate: productivity, decomposition, infiltration, thermal regulation, nutrient cycles or population dynamics? An intervention may improve appearance without restoring these functions.

Ecological integrity

Integrity combines composition, structure and function against suitable reference conditions. It does not require turning an air base into a pristine area; it requires defining what condition is feasible and desirable in context, which pressures must decline and how change will be verified.

Theory 2.3

Connectivity, fragmentation and edge effects

Fragmentation is more than loss of area. It divides continuous habitat into small, isolated patches through roads, fences, lighting, buildings or land-use change. Isolation restricts movement, genetic exchange and recolonisation. It also increases edge effects: greater exposure to light, noise, temperature change, predation and opportunistic species.

Structural connectivity describes whether landscape elements appear connected; functional connectivity asks whether a species can actually use them. A vegetated strip may be a corridor for insects and a barrier to other wildlife. Corridors must therefore be designed for target species or processes, not as generic features.

Aerospace sites face a legitimate tension between connectivity and wildlife-hazard management. Attracting certain species near a runway can increase aircraft-strike risk. The answer is not “more biodiversity everywhere”, but zoning: conserve and connect where compatible, avoid attractants in critical areas and coordinate ecology with operational safety.

Resistance

The capacity to withstand a disturbance without substantial change. It depends on the event's intensity, duration and frequency and on the system's prior condition.

Recovery

The capacity to return to a functional condition after disturbance. A system may have low resistance but recover well; both concepts form part of resilience.

Theory 2.4

Selecting indicators without misleading yourself

An indicator is useful when it answers a management question. To assess whether less frequent mowing improves pollinator habitat, measure flowering-plant cover and diversity, visits by pollinator groups and continuity of resources through time. Counting volunteer days measures activity, not ecological outcome.

  • Pressure indicators: sealed area, light intensity, mowing frequency, noise or pollutant concentration.
  • State indicators: habitat condition, native cover, abundance of sensitive groups, connectivity or soil quality.
  • Response indicators: actions delivered, budget, protocols implemented or personnel trained.
  • Outcome indicators: observed change against baseline and objective.

A single visit rarely supports attribution. Seasonality, annual weather, detectability and survey effort must be recorded. Comparing equivalent periods and retaining the method are as important as choosing the metric.

Theory 2.5 · Deep dive

Scale, time and detectability: why one visit is not enough

Observed biodiversity is only part of the biodiversity present. Method determines what can be concluded.

Spatial scale

The question defines the scale. Assessing a plot requires microhabitat and local-condition data; understanding connectivity expands analysis to the landscape; a migratory species may depend on routes and sites hundreds of kilometres away. An administrative map supports management but does not delimit the ecological system.

The occupied area must also be distinguished from the area of influence. Light, noise, runoff, dust or wildlife movement can extend effects beyond a project's physical footprint.

Temporal scale and phenology

Many species are not detectable all year. Breeding, migration, flowering, insect emergence and temporary-wetland use follow different calendars. A winter survey does not exclude spring breeding, and a dry spring may not represent an average year.

The baseline should cover decision-relevant periods and record weather and effort. When the programme cannot cover a full cycle, the limitation must be explicit and precautionary conditions designed.

Detectability

Failure to observe a species does not prove absence. Detection probability depends on observer expertise, time, weather, method, duration and behaviour. Repeated visits, combined techniques and documented effort reduce false negatives.

Existing maps, inventories, citizen-science records or historical data can guide work, but must be reviewed for date, resolution and bias. They do not automatically replace a project-specific survey.

Populations and metapopulations

A local population may depend on others through dispersal and recolonisation. Connected patches form a metapopulation: some produce individuals while others serve as temporary areas. Losing a seemingly small patch may disrupt exchange and increase local-extinction risk.

Management should ask whether the action reduces area, quality or permeability and whether safe passages remain sufficient. A “corridor” is functional only if it serves the needs of target species.

Reading evidence correctly

A professional conclusion takes one of three forms: sufficient evidence to decide; insufficient evidence requiring study before a decision; or a conditional decision with conservative controls, monitoring and stop thresholds. Treating a lack of observations as absence of ecological value is an unjustified inference.

The aerospace life cycle and biodiversity

A design change can reduce one pressure while increasing another. Life cycle thinking reveals shifts among locations, suppliers and stages before a decision is made.

Key idea

Comparison requires a shared function

A lighter part is not automatically better, nor is a recyclable one. First define the function, service period, operating conditions and safety requirements. Then compare equivalent alternatives.

Module rule

Do not optimise one stage by sacrificing another without understanding the scale and reason for the trade-off.

Theory 3.1

What life cycle thinking contributes

Function and functional unit

The function expresses the service delivered: protecting electronics for a defined life, transmitting a load, controlling a surface or maintaining an operational capability. The functional unit quantifies that service so equivalent alternatives can be compared.

Comparing one kilogram of two materials can mislead if one needs more mass, lasts less time or cannot be repaired. For a retrofit, the unit may include service hours, required availability and expected replacements.

System boundaries

Boundaries define included processes: extraction and refining, manufacture, transport, assembly, operation, maintenance, upgrade, retirement and final treatment. Excluding a critical stage may reverse the conclusion.

Geographical and temporal boundaries also matter. Biodiversity is highly location-dependent: the same occupied area or water use has different meaning according to ecological sensitivity and local scarcity.

Inventory and data quality

The inventory collects materials, energy, emissions, transport and treatment. For biodiversity, these flows connect to mechanisms such as land transformation, extraction, pollution or resource use.

Supplier data are preferable when traceable and comparable. Averages, proxies and assumptions must be declared. Many decimal places do not create precision when the source is weak.

Assessment and interpretation

Methods translate inventories into impact categories. Major uncertainties remain for biodiversity, particularly for local impacts and complex chains, so results must not be presented as absolute truth.

Interpretation identifies dominant processes, sensitivity to assumptions and robust decisions. Sometimes the correct conclusion is to request specific data or run a pilot rather than select immediately.

Theory 3.2

An aircraft impact map

A400M and Eurofighter provide the visual and operational context. These examples are generic and do not describe confidential configurations or supply chains.

1. Raw materials

Metals, minerals, polymers, rubber and composites begin outside the plant. Extraction may convert habitat, fragment landscapes, alter water, generate mining waste or open access routes. Severity depends on location and management, not only the material name.

2. Transformation and manufacture

Refining, surface treatment, machining, curing, electronics and assembly use substances and resources and generate residual flows. Management-system controls reduce direct risks; eco-design can reduce mass, scrap and separation complexity.

3. Logistics and packaging

Supplier sites, routes, logistics centres and packaging are part of the system. Untreated wood can move organisms; urgency can multiply transport; well-managed returnable packaging reduces material demand and introduction risk.

4. Operation and support

Bases, runways, testing, lighting, maintenance and replacement interact with soil, water and wildlife. Operational availability may require redundancy and stock, while predictive maintenance, repair and planning reduce avoidable replacements and transport.

5. Retrofit and life extension

Updating systems can retain much of the platform's embedded value, but also introduces new materials and components. Benefit depends on added life, reliability, compatibility, repairability and the destination of removed equipment.

6. End of life

Disassembly supported by material information enables component recovery, alloy separation and substance management. “Recyclable” describes technical potential; real outcomes depend on access, purity, volume, markets, traceability and treatment capacity.

Theory 3.3

Four ways to shift the problem

Between impacts

Reducing mass with a material linked to more sensitive extraction can improve one indicator and worsen another. The trade-off must be recognised and disproportionate burdens avoided.

Between stages

More complex manufacture may be offset by longer life — or not. A coating with greater initial burden may reduce maintenance, but it must be assessed against hazard, repair and end of life.

Between locations

Outsourcing does not eliminate impact. It may transfer it to a less-controlled catchment, more sensitive habitat or suppliers with weaker data capacity.

Between generations

A short-lived or hard-to-disassemble solution may cut today's cost while creating future dependency, waste and loss of critical materials. Time horizon is part of engineering.

Theory 3.4

Eco-design decision gates

  • Need: can the function be delivered through software, repair or replacement of only a submodule?
  • Architecture: is the solution modular, accessible and upgradeable without removing full assemblies?
  • Materials: are composition, origin, substances and supply-chain sensitivity known?
  • Manufacture: what scrap, processes and controls are required?
  • Service: what reliability, inspection, repair and logistics are needed over the planned life?
  • End of life: can fractions be disassembled, identified and routed realistically?
  • Evidence: does the comparison use the same function, scenario and data quality?

These questions do not replace design; they enrich it so retained value and nature-related pressures are considered while architecture can still change.

Theory 3.5 · Deep dive

What a life cycle assessment can — and cannot — demonstrate

The method structures information and reveals trade-offs, but conclusion quality depends on purpose, boundaries and data.

Screening versus comparative study

A screening uses secondary data and conservative assumptions to identify hotspots and focus investigation. It suits early design while architecture and suppliers can change. A comparative study supporting an external claim needs stronger functional equivalence, assumption review and representative data.

Not every decision needs equal precision. Maturity should be proportionate to the risk of error and the decision's reversibility.

Biodiversity in LCA

Expressing biodiversity in one unit is difficult because damage depends on location, habitat, initial condition, species and time. Models estimate potential impacts linked to land use, pollution, climate change or resource use, but do not replace local ecological assessment.

Robust practice combines life cycle indicators for global chains with spatial studies for priority sites and suppliers. Aggregation should retain the information explaining each hotspot.

Data hierarchy and quality

Primary data are preferred for processes that dominate results or distinguish options: repair performance, real replacement rate, composition, refining location or process consumption. Generic data can serve minor elements where geographically and temporally reasonable.

Record source, year, technology, geography, uncertainty and owner. A justified range may be more reliable than a number with many decimals.

Sensitivity analysis

Recalculate with alternative assumptions for life, failure rate, upgrades, transport, energy, recycled content or final recovery. If a small change reverses the recommendation, the conclusion is fragile and needs better data or conditional approval.

If it holds under reasonable scenarios, the choice is robust despite uncertainty. Transparency about stability is more useful than declaring an absolute winner.

Recommended design gate

Before architecture freeze, document: function and service horizon; failure and upgrade scenarios; masses and critical materials; disassembly strategy; repair availability; relevant suppliers and locations; end-of-life assumptions; main trade-offs; and uncertain data that could change selection. The final decision integrates sustainability with airworthiness, safety, cost, schedule and performance.

Operations, land and wildlife

Local management must protect operational safety, compliance, mission continuity and the surrounding area's ecological condition at the same time.

Key idea

Compatibility, not decoration

A base may contain ecological functions, corridors and sensitive receptors. Yet an action that improves habitat far from the runway may be an unsuitable attractant in a critical zone. Quality means placing each measure where it performs its function without creating new risk.

Module rule

Manage pressure at source and design space around receptors and functions.

Theory 4.1

Build a territorial baseline

Physical map

Include topography, slopes, soils, drainage, flood zones and permeable and sealed surfaces. These determine how water, sediment and pollutants move and which areas are vulnerable to works or incidents.

Ecological map

Map habitats, condition, relevant species or groups, corridors, breeding areas and links to external areas. Record survey season and effort; non-detection after limited effort does not prove absence.

Operational map

Locate runways, vehicle routes, engine tests, lighting, storage, drains, work areas, schedules and contingencies. This enables pressures to be overlaid with receptors.

Constraints map

Integrate legal requirements, easements, safety, fire protection, emergency access and technical limitations. An ecological measure that blocks a critical function is not viable.

Theory 4.2

Source, pathway and receptor

A source generates pressure: an engine test, luminaire, work surface or chemical. The pathway explains how it travels: air, water, soil, acoustic propagation, direct line of sight or carriage on equipment. The receptor is the exposed element: an aquatic community, breeding habitat, nocturnal fauna or functioning soil.

If no complete pathway exists, risk may be low; multiple pathways or sensitive receptors increase it. Controls should act first at source — change timing, reduce intensity, contain — then on the pathway — barriers, drainage, distance — and finally at the receptor. This avoids reliance on fragile end-of-pipe measures.

Significance combines magnitude, extent, duration, frequency, reversibility and receptor sensitivity. Probability is not importance: an infrequent event still requires control where consequences are severe or irreversible.

Theory 4.3

Frequent operational pressures

Noise and vibration

Response depends on species, frequency, level, duration, predictability and life stage. Breeding, migration or rest can be sensitive. Annual averages may hide relevant short peaks, so protocols must consider event and season.

Artificial light at night

Intensity, spectrum, direction and duration affect orientation, feeding and rhythms. Light only where, when and as much as necessary, with shielding and zone-based management compatible with safety.

Soil and drainage

Compaction, excavation and sealing change infiltration and connectivity. Runoff can carry solids or substances. Separate clean and potentially contaminated water, protect stockpiles and maintain barriers before rainfall.

Movement and invasive species

Vehicles, equipment, soils and packaging may transport seeds, invertebrates or pathogens. Inspection, cleaning and construction-material traceability close introduction pathways.

Theory 4.4

Wildlife and aviation safety: integrated management

Wildlife-strike risk requires reducing the presence and movement of hazardous species in critical zones. This does not mean eliminating all site biodiversity. It means identifying attractants — standing water, food, shelter or certain vegetation — understanding patterns and responding proportionately.

Zoning separates objectives: close to runways and flight paths, prioritise habitat unattractive to risk species; in distant compatible areas, conserve valuable habitat, improve connectivity or restore functions. Use sightings and incident records, species identification, timing, weather and continuous measure evaluation.

Deterrence, habitat modification and operational response must be coordinated. An isolated measure may shift wildlife into a more dangerous area. Evaluate success through reduced presence and risk in critical zones, not the number of interventions.

Theory 4.5 · Deep dive

From map to daily operational plan

A baseline creates value only when it changes planning, work permits, incident response and team coordination.

Functional zoning

Divide the base by compatibility and risk: critical operational areas; attractant-control zones; transition areas; compatible corridors or habitats; drainage and downstream receptors; and temporary work surfaces. Each zone needs permitted uses, restrictions and an owner.

Zoning must appear in actual operational systems: drawings, work orders, contractor briefings and campaign planning. A map stored in a report controls nothing.

Time windows

Pressure can be acceptable in one period and unsuitable in another. Breeding, migration, rain, drought, wind and nocturnal activity change exposure. Moving a test to another window may reduce risk without changing its technical objective.

Restrictions should specify dates, times, weather and exception criteria. Where urgent operations require deviation, document the rationale and activate additional controls.

Integrated wildlife management

Aviation safety requires knowledge of species, abundance, movement, height, timing and attractants. Combine habitat, water, food and structure management with observation and appropriate deterrence. Removing wildlife without addressing attractants is usually temporary.

The goal is not maximum biodiversity beside a runway, but fewer hazardous encounters and conservation in compatible locations. Safety and conservation both require spatial and temporal data.

Change and temporary work

Construction, stockpiles, temporary lighting, trenches, soil movement and packaging create pressures unlike stable operations. Integrate environmental control into management of change: prior review, contractor requirements, inspection, closure and restoration.

Control biosecurity through machinery cleaning, soil and plant origin, wood packaging and early surveillance for invasive species. Prevention is more effective than eradicating an established population.

Thresholds and stop-work authority

An operational plan defines when to proceed, correct or stop: failed sediment barriers, unexpected sensitive fauna, out-of-limit illuminance, blocked drainage or weather that invalidates controls. Personnel must know who decides and how work restarts safely.

Module 5 · 20–30 minutes

Materials, procurement and circularity

A component's biodiversity footprint begins long before it reaches the plant. Traceability, service life and value retention are engineering and procurement decisions.

TraceabilityDue diligenceCircularitySuppliers
05 / 08
Objectives

By the end, you will be able to

01Map a material chain beyond the direct supplier.
02Distinguish traceability from risk-based due diligence.
03Apply a value-retention hierarchy to aerospace equipment.
04Translate expectations into verifiable contractual requirements.
Key idea

The direct supplier is not the origin

A Tier 1 supplier may know performance and price while extraction, refining and conversion remain several tiers away. Biodiversity risk often concentrates where the organisation has least visibility.

Module rule

Do not treat a certificate as the end of the analysis: verify scope, chain and risk response.

Theory 5.1

From deposit to component

Extraction

Mining and raw-material production can transform habitat, fragment landscapes, alter water and create long-lived waste. Significance depends on location, ecological sensitivity, methods and controls.

Concentration and refining

Ore becomes a usable material through water-, energy- and chemical-intensive processes. The mine's country is not necessarily the refining location; both matter.

Industrial conversion

Smelting, alloying, polymerisation and precursor manufacture determine composition and influence later repair, separation and recovery.

Integration

Design choices set mass, reliability, replaceable units, fastening, access and information. They can lock future material demand in or out for years.

Theory 5.2

Traceability is not due diligence

Traceability

Traceability answers who, where and when: entities, facilities, batches and chain of custody. It reveals what is known and where information stops.

Due diligence

Due diligence identifies and assesses actual or potential harm, prevents and mitigates it, tracks effectiveness, communicates and provides or supports remedy where appropriate.

Sufficient evidence

Review the certificate's scope, certified entity, period, method, exclusions and link to the supplied material. A generic policy is weaker than installation-level evidence.

Escalation

Missing information triggers a proportionate action: clarification, improvement plan, audit, conditional approval, redesign or, where severe harm cannot be addressed, disengagement.

Theory 5.3

Circularity: retain value before shredding it

Avoid and reduce

Question the need, reduce scrap and unnecessary mass, and simplify material diversity without compromising safety.

Maintain, repair and upgrade

Keeping function generally retains more embedded value than material recovery. Access, diagnostics, documentation and authorised repair are design requirements.

Reuse and remanufacture

Reuse needs demonstrated condition, configuration control and a valid route back into service. Remanufacture restores defined performance through controlled processes.

Recycle

Recycling is important for true end of life, but depends on identification, disassembly, purity, volume, markets and treatment capability.

Theory 5.4

What responsible procurement should request

  • Material composition and controlled-substance information.
  • Chain mapping to the tier where material risk becomes meaningful.
  • Facilities, countries and changes in origin.
  • Evidence of habitat-conversion, water, waste and incident controls.
  • Flow-down requirements, records and audit rights.
  • Repair, spare-part, return and end-of-service arrangements.
  • Corrective-action milestones and escalation rules.

Price, mass and lead time remain necessary. The aim is a better multi-criteria decision, not replacing engineering requirements with an environmental label.

Theory 5.5 · Deep dive

Due diligence that reaches the contract

Collecting documents is only one step. The process must identify risk, influence the commercial relationship and verify effectiveness.

1. Map and prioritise

Identify entities, facilities and countries through transformation, refining and, where material, extraction or recovery. Prioritise by material, volume, substitutability, location sensitivity, history and leverage. A billing country is not physical origin.

2. Assess evidence

Test document scope, chain of custody, period, method and exceptions. A PDF does not reduce risk by itself; effectiveness depends on whether the system prevents conversion, manages water and mining waste, controls incidents and supports remedy.

3. Prevent and mitigate

Use origin specifications, verified secondary content, alternative materials, yield improvement, traceability, supplier support or redesign. Immediate exit can shift harm and remove leverage; unconditional continuation is also unacceptable.

4. Contract and monitor

Flow requirements to sub-tiers, require change notification and records, retain audit rights, define action plans, repair data, take-back and consequences. Monitor both traceability coverage and effective risk closure.

Circular design in aerospace

Circularity integrates safety and certification through accessible modules, stable interfaces, reversible fastening where suitable, substance and material documentation, diagnostics, spares, authorised repair, compatible upgrades and real recovery routes. A merely “recyclable” option may be inferior to one that avoids replacements for fifteen years.

Module 6 · 20–30 minutes

Mitigation hierarchy and restoration

Avoid, reduce and restore; consider compensation only for residual impacts under strict conditions.

AvoidReduceRestoreResidual
06 / 08
Objectives

By the end, you will be able to

01Apply the hierarchy in the correct order.
02Distinguish rehabilitation, restoration and compensation.
03Design objectives, trajectories and corrective thresholds.
04Challenge additionality, leakage, permanence and time lag.
Key idea

Restoration does not authorise destruction

Avoidance retains function from day one. Restoration is uncertain and may take decades; it is not a shortcut around viable alternatives.

Module rule

Demonstrate avoidance before describing restoration benefits.

Theory 6.1

The response sequence

1. Avoid

Change location, footprint, timing, technology or need so the impact does not occur. Avoidance is strongest where values are sensitive or irreplaceable.

2. Reduce

Control unavoidable pressures at source and along pathways: smaller footprint, different schedule, shielding, containment or less intense operation.

3. Restore or rehabilitate

Repair affected composition, structure and function against a defined reference and trajectory. Planting alone is an activity, not proof of recovery.

4. Residual impacts

Characterise what remains after feasible measures. Some critical or irreplaceable impacts may be unacceptable rather than compensable.

Theory 6.2

Restoration as a managed process

Causal diagnosis

Remove the pressure — compaction, altered drainage, invasives or disturbance — before treating symptoms.

Reference and objective

Define feasible desired condition using habitat, soil, hydrology, species and operational constraints.

Trajectory and thresholds

Set milestones for cover, composition, structure, function and pressure reduction. Thresholds trigger corrective action.

Adaptive management

Monitor, learn and modify methods. Adaptation is planned management of uncertainty, not an admission of failure.

Theory 6.3

Nature-based solutions, with conditions

A nature-based solution addresses a social challenge while benefiting biodiversity through ecosystem protection, management or restoration. A decorative planter is not automatically one. On industrial sites, vegetated drainage, soil restoration, shade, thermal regulation or compatible connectivity may be useful, but design starts with the problem and evaluates effectiveness, space, maintenance, wildlife risk and future climate.

Theory 6.4

Four credibility tests

Additionality

Would the gain occur without the intervention?

Leakage

Has the pressure moved elsewhere?

Permanence

Who funds and maintains the gain for as long as the impact lasts?

Time lag

Immediate loss and slow recovery are not equal hectares.

Theory 6.5 · Deep dive

Residual impact and ecological equivalence

Characterise the residual

Quantify area, condition, function, connectivity, affected species, duration, reversibility and uncertainty after all feasible controls.

Equivalence

Trees do not automatically replace grassland. Compare ecosystem type, composition, structure, function, location and benefit to affected receptors.

Gain before loss

Begin recovery early and account conservatively for time debt and failure risk.

Adaptive follow-up

Measure survival, composition, functions, invasives and pressures; trigger correction when trajectory fails.

Prudent language

“No net loss” and “nature positive” are balance claims requiring scope, method, equivalence, additionality, permanence and verification. A local improvement must not be presented as neutralising a base, programme or company's total impact.

Measure and communicate with rigour

Evidence turns good intentions into manageable decisions. Responsible communication states scope, method, result, uncertainty and limitations.

Key idea

Measurement is not collecting numbers

A good system selects a few variables linked to the impact mechanism, assigns action when they change and retains data traceability.

Module rule

If an indicator changes no decision, reconsider why it is measured.

Theory 7.1

The evidence chain

Objective

Define desired condition, scope and deadline.

Pressure and mechanism

Identify what changes and how it reaches the receptor.

State and outcome

State describes condition; outcome describes change consistent with the intervention.

Decision

Set thresholds for continuation, correction, investigation or stop.

Theory 7.2

Activity is not impact

Input

Budget, hours, contracts and equipment show capacity.

Activity and output

Surveys, treated hectares, changed lights and assessed suppliers show delivery.

Outcome

Reduced off-site light, improved native cover or closed traceability gaps show short- to medium-term change.

Impact

Long-term biodiversity change requires monitoring, comparison and caution about attribution.

Theory 7.3

Baseline, method and uncertainty

Baseline

Represent relevant periods and record method, weather, season and effort.

Sampling design

Location, replication, frequency and detectability determine quality.

Data quality

Assess precision, completeness, representativeness, consistency and traceability.

Uncertainty

State sources and effects; use ranges, scenarios and sensitivity analysis.

Theory 7.4

Compatible frameworks

ESRS E4

Structures material biodiversity impacts, dependencies, risks, opportunities, policies, actions, targets and metrics under the applicable reporting framework.

TNFD and LEAP

Locate interfaces, Evaluate dependencies and impacts, Assess risks and opportunities, and Prepare responses and disclosure.

Global Biodiversity Framework

Target 15 addresses business assessment and disclosure of nature-related dependencies, impacts and risks.

ISO 14001

Turns analysis into context, aspects, controls, objectives, competence, evaluation and improvement.

Theory 7.5

Communication resistant to greenwashing

  • Specific: name action, place, period and variable.
  • Proportionate: do not present a local gain as the whole programme's solution.
  • Verifiable: link method, baseline, source and owner.
  • Complete: state relevant limitations and adverse effects.
  • Time-correct: do not present targets or activities as achieved impacts.
  • Comparable: state denominator, base year, scope and method changes.
Theory 7.6 · Deep dive

Indicator governance and assurance

Technical sheet

Define question, unit, scope, period, source, method, frequency, owner, minimum quality and limitations.

Baseline and reference

Baseline is the starting condition; reference is the desired ecological condition. They may differ.

Data controls

Record method changes, investigate anomalies and retain originals for audit.

Threshold and action

Define what happens when the indicator improves, stabilises or deteriorates.

Claim test

Ask: what scope and period, which variable and method, which baseline, what is measured versus estimated, what is excluded, and can a third party reconstruct the calculation?

Regulatory note

Reporting frameworks and timetables change. Always verify legal applicability, transition periods and corporate scope against current rules and procedures.
